# Blog post workflow

Use this when moving from a thought to a polished post.

## 1. Capture

Write the raw thought without trying to sound good.

Required notes:

- What happened?
- What annoyed, surprised, or taught me?
- What do I believe now?
- What would a strong engineer disagree with?
- What example proves I have touched the problem?

Do not outline yet. Get the material down first.

## 2. Distill

Turn the raw thought into a one-sentence claim.

Good claims:

- "Most MCPs are trust boundaries, not interface breakthroughs."
- "Great software design often looks underwhelming because it removes risk instead of adding architecture."
- "Writing matters to engineers because unclear prose is usually unclear thought."

Weak claims:

- "Some thoughts on MCPs."
- "AI is changing software."
- "Writing is important."

If the claim cannot be stated sharply, keep thinking.

## 3. Choose The Shape

Pick one structure.

### Argument Essay

Use when you want to change what the reader believes.

1. Concrete spark.
2. Thesis.
3. Mechanism.
4. Example.
5. Objection.
6. Practical stance.
7. Ending.

### Field Note

Use when you learned something from a tool, bug, or workflow.

1. What I was trying to do.
2. What actually happened.
3. What I misunderstood.
4. What changed my mind.
5. What I would do next time.

### Craft Essay

Use when writing about engineering taste.

1. Name the habit.
2. Show why juniors miss it.
3. Show how seniors see it.
4. Give concrete examples.
5. Define the boundary.
6. End with a rule of thumb.

### Personal Technical Essay

Use when the post starts from your own growth.

1. Scene.
2. Old belief.
3. Collision with reality.
4. Technical lesson.
5. Current belief.
6. Personal landing.

## 4. Outline

Use section headings as the argument skeleton.

Each heading must answer one question:

- What is the claim?
- Why does it happen?
- What does it look like in practice?
- What goes wrong?
- What should we do?
- Where does the claim stop?

Delete headings that only label a topic.

## 5. Draft

Draft quickly. Keep momentum.

Rules:

- Put the thesis high.
- Use the best example early.
- Do not save all the concrete detail for the middle.
- Let paragraphs be short.
- Leave ugly sentences in place if the idea is moving.

## 6. First Edit: Structure

Read only for shape.

Checklist:

- Does paragraph 1 create a real reason to read?
- Does the thesis appear by paragraph 4?
- Does each section do a different job?
- Is the strongest example early enough?
- Is there a real objection?
- Does the ending land somewhere specific?

Move blocks before editing sentences.

## 7. Second Edit: Force

Read only for authority.

Replace:

- "I think X might be true" with "X is true when Y."
- "There are many reasons" with the actual reasons.
- "This is useful" with who uses it and what it changes.
- "It depends" with the conditions it depends on.
- "A lot" with a number, scope, or concrete comparison.

## 8. Third Edit: Sound

Read aloud.

Cut:

- Filler adverbs.
- Repeated setup.
- Soft transitions.
- Sentences that explain your intent instead of making the point.
- Any paragraph that repeats the previous paragraph with different words.

Keep:

- Specific nouns.
- Sharp verbs.
- Concrete examples.
- Honest uncertainty.
- A little personality when it comes from contact with the material.

## 9. Publish Check

Before moving a draft to `page.mdx`:

- Metadata has title, description, tags, hero, and date.
- Metadata export ends with `};`.
- Title names the claim or object.
- Description states the payoff.
- Links point to primary sources when possible.
- The post does not depend on private context the reader cannot inspect.
- The ending is not a generic recap.

## 10. Post-Publish Review

After publishing, ask:

- Which paragraph would I quote?
- Which paragraph would a skeptical engineer challenge?
- Which example made the post feel real?
- Which sentence sounds like filler?
- What should the next post answer?
